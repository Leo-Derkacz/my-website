Paracelsus

Paracelsus was a German-Swiss physician known as the father of toxicology, born in Switzerland, in 1493. In 1510, he received a baccalaureate medicine degree from the University of Vienna, and in 1516 received a doctorate from the University of Ferrara. In that time, he went from university to university, disappointed in each one and frustrated with their traditional ways of education, famously remarking “the high colleges managed to produce so many high asses,” showing how he prefers unorthodox and unique education. In 1530, he made a clinical description of syphilis, which changed how the disease was viewed, making shockwaves in the medical world. 6 years later, he published the Great Surgery Book, which elevated how doctors viewed surgery, and changed medical practice forever. His assertion “the dose makes the poison” is still used in toxicology today. [3]

"The dose makes the poison" is such a useful phrase because it highlights how, in Paracelsus's time and ours, people treat/treated a chemical based on how known it is rather than the actual danger level.

For example, Ben and Jerry's ice cream was sued in 2017 for having trace amounts of glyphosate, a dangerous pesticide, in their ice cream. However, Neil DeGrasse Tyson, the award-winning author and scientist, debunked this thoroughly. The issue with the claim was that it would take 30 million pints to reach the LD50, or the dose at which 50% of people would die. The LD50 of glyphosate is not much higher than salt! What's more, eating 30 pints of their ice cream would kill you, not because of any scary-sounding chemicals, but because of the sugar.

This ordeal prompted one commenter to joke that bananas should be banned, seeing as eating 40,000 per hour would kill you with radiation poisoning. Clearly, Paracelsus's ideas are now not only still relevant, but perhaps more relevant than ever before.

Quick Facts

  • The Bubonic Plague, also called the Black Death, was a sweeping pandemic across Europe that killed an estimated 25-50 million people, almost half of Europe's population at the time.
  • It is widely considered the deadliest pandemic in history (when adjusted as percentage of global population).
  • The bacterium that was responsible for the sickness was called Yersinia Pestis, a pathogen also responsible for the Plague of Justinian, another deadly pandemic some 800 years before the Black Death.
  • Although it mainly originated and spread through rats and other vermin, the explanations people at the time came up with ranged anywhere from astrology to antisemitism.
  • On that note, one of the main things we will be dicussing about the Plague is the role of antisemitism in the Black Death.

Antisemitism in the Plague

Before people knew about germs and contaminants, they had to come up with the cause for disease themselves. This invariably leads to religion - the idea that it is some kind of religious punishment, OR that it is due to others with differing religions punishing them.

This meant Jewish people were largely a scapegoat for a near-fully Christian Europe at the time. One Oxford Academic Journal describes how Jews were accused of poisoning food, wells and streams, tortured into confessions, rounded up in city squares or their synagogues, and exterminated en masse. The largely Christian majority used religion as a way to blame a certain group for their misfortune. [1]

Unfortunately, this has become a common social precedent in history - if something goes wrong, blame the Jews.

How does this affect our world today?

In terms of antisemitism, the idea of blaming Jews for simply existing is one that would repeat itself throughout history, like in the Holocaust through World War II. It is vitally important for us both as a society and as individuals to recognize our biases and not repeat history.

In terms of the actual plague, the number of cases and geographical reach have been slowly increasing across the past hundred years. With the rise of movements like MAHA, people have become increasingly resistant in taking antibiotics and vaccines. [2]

We need to keep trusting things like antibiotics and vaccines. While they have their issues, like antibiotic resistance and killing healthy gut bacteria, wild claims about autism and government brain chips are simply self-sabotage.

Bernardino Ramazzini

Bernardino Ramazzini was often considered the "father of occupational medicine." While studying medicine at Parma University, he was among the first to investigate worker safety and illness, interviewing them about their practices, observing their activities, and discussing their illnesses. Ramazzini collected these findings into a book, De Morbis Artificum Diatriba [Diseases of Workers], which he used as the basis for his medical teaching in the future. [4]

Ramazzini revolutionized malaria treatment, as his Britannica article notes that as “a strong proponent of the use of cinchona bark in the treatment of malaria, Ramazzini recognized the introduction of this medicament (from which the alkaloid quinine is derived) as a revolutionary event in the history of medicine.” The word they use - revolutionize - is an accurate description of how much he defined the field of occupational medicine, something that still very much affects us, if not defines us, today.

He is such a highly respected scientist because he investigated actual lower-classes worker safety when othe doctors and physicians at the time conisdered themselves to high-class and pretentious for that.

Ramazzini’s trailblazing is described in countless modern applications - one NIH research article described how “occupational medicine, an interdisciplinary field that combines knowledge from public health, organizational psychology, and people management, can develop initiatives to mitigate the negative effects of work activities while fostering healthier and more productive work environments.” [8] The article explicitly mentions Ramazzini elsewhere and how he almost singlehandedly created this vitally important sector of medicine.

John Graunt

John Graunt was an English merchant and haberdasher by trade who delved into health and became the father of demography, the study of statistics such as births, deaths, income, or the incidence of disease, which illustrate the changing structure of human populations. Born in London in 1620, Graunt lived in a period where a lot of public health answers needed answers, and at some point in the mid 1600s, researched the London death records, and made some interesting findings about the phenomena of death statistics. Upon these findings, he published the Natural and Political Observations . . . Made upon the Bills of Mortality in 1662. In this book, he revealed his findings, including how males had a higher birth rate than females, although their mortality rates were also higher, so it offset. [5] [7]

Demography is not just an obscure and lost social science - it is one of the foundations of civilization today. One Why Social Science? article wrote that "Demography is crucial for community planning and resilience. Understanding population trends, migration patterns, and shifts in household composition allows planners to anticipate future needs for housing, infrastructure, and land use. For example, an area with an aging population might need to expand on-demand transportation services, accessible housing, and more frequent public transit. A community facing population decline might need to focus on adapting land-use policies or consolidating services to reflect changing needs." [9]

As this example illustrates, demography is the very foundation for how populations are tracked and cared for today in our modern world.

Galileo Galilei and The Rennaisance

Galileo Galilei is widely considered one of the most influential scientists ever, being credited as the "father of modern science." His role in shaping how modern science works today was essential to the Rennaisance, a period between the 14th and 17th centuries in which Europe went through a revolution so to speak of art, science, and mathematics.

He helped develop the scientific method that we know and love today, and that almost every single middle and high schooler since is familiar with through their science classes. One of his groundbreaking ideas was that he believed "the book of nature was written in the language of mathematics [which] changed natural philosophy from a verbal, qualitative account to a mathematical one in which experimentation became a recognized method for discovering the facts of nature," as his Britannica article notes.

Summary, Glossary, Game, and Bibliography

Summary

To conclude, these five figures and/or time periods demonstrate the foundation of our modern understanding of science as it is. Here are some of the key ideas and modern applications we can glean from their experiences:

  • Be critical and skeptical of the current status quo
  • Do not repeat existing biases in history
  • Investigate the things that may seem insignificant to others to discover the real magic
  • Anyone can do science, even a fabric merchant, so don't gatekeep it
  • Change the world for the better! Make it so future generations will never forget you

Through these five lessons, we learn that historical analysis is all but required in understanding our future, our past, and everything around us in the present.
